- Your business throws off more cash than you can deploy

## Core frameworks

### 1. Wealth is a ratio, not a number

The load-bearing idea of the whole module. Absolute dollars tell you nothing about
whether someone is wealthy; the ratio between what they have and what they need does.
He states it directly in `t7o8dtUWPQg` as **wealth is a ratio, not a number**.

The consequence is that the fastest route to feeling wealthy is shrinking the
denominator, not growing the numerator:

- **Price is a share of the buyer's wallet, not a figure.** A $50 purchase is 50% of a $100 net worth; under 1% of a rich person's net worth — the same sticker price is a different decision for different buyers. [m-k0_pQJ1fY](https://youtu.be/m-k0_pQJ1fY)
- **Ultra-wealthy status spending is trivial relative to net worth.** $300 million yachts vs $100 billion — less than 1% [m-k0_pQJ1fY](https://youtu.be/m-k0_pQJ1fY)
- **Percentage growth scales with the base, which is why the rich get richer.** 10% of $1M is $100,000; 10% of $1B is $100 million [wtsX7WHQMFM](https://youtu.be/wtsX7WHQMFM)

**Use it:** when a goal feels unreachable, check which side of the ratio you are
trying to move. Most people attack the numerator by earning more, which is slow and
capped, when the denominator is the cheaper lever.

### 2. The four capital structures

A 2x2 of *whose money* against *whose business* — the clearest map he gives of how
capital moves, from `BO_59sGxztY`:

| | Your business | Other people's businesses |
|---|---|---|
| **Your money** | Bootstrap | Invest |
| **Other people's money** | Raise funding | Run a fund |

His claim is that the **fund-manager path dominates** — it is the only structure
where other people's money is applied to other people's businesses and you keep a
share of the upside. The arithmetic he works live in `BO_59sGxztY` and again in
`sL16tsGafcQ`: a **$5M GP commitment** (5% of a $100M fund) controls **$300M of
businesses** via $200M of debt; after debt service, LP capital and a preferred
return, 20% carry leaves a **$100M slice** from the original $5M.

His own line on it: *the fund manager will often make more money than any of the founders of the businesses they buy* — [BO_59sGxztY](https://youtu.be/BO_59sGxztY)

**The prerequisites are the hard part**, and he names only two: *proprietary deal
flow* (deals nobody else sees, so there is no auction) and an *edge in the industry*.

**`Ignorance debt`** is the reason he recommends first-time founders bootstrap: the tuition the universe charges for not knowing what you are doing. Pay it down on your own money, not your friends' and family's. [BO_59sGxztY](https://youtu.be/BO_59sGxztY)

### 3. The ownership ladder

From `Fy8XX8EuEnA` — a five-rung ladder, and the point is that each rung requires
you to give up doing the thing that got you there:

| Rung | What you do |
|---|---|
| Self-employed | Doing things for money |
| Manager | Managing people who do things |
| Leader | Leading managers |
| Executive | Leading organisational change around objectives |
| Above the business | Nothing in the business; advising, allocating capital |

**Scale zero** is the mechanism that forces the climb: if value delivery requires
you to be present, growth stops the moment the team outgrows your capacity to deliver
it. You peel your own skills out of the business one at a time until the only thing
left for you is the biggest decisions.

**Who not what** is the replacement logic — and he attaches a qualifier that is easy
to miss: you can only hire the *who* once you have learned the *what* yourself,
otherwise you cannot evaluate them. The people you want arrive *batteries included*,
having already solved the problem elsewhere.

### 4. Every business incurs debt at incorporation

The frame that dissolves the bootstrap-vs-raise argument. Bootstrapping does not
avoid debt — it **converts financial debt into talent debt, technological debt and
ignorance debt.** The question was never *whether* to take on debt, only *which type*.

Related, from `52tcB5FopAg` and `yr1DrcPCKEg`: **debt is a de-risking event** when it
takes money off the table. A dividend recap or a minority sale converts illiquid
concentrated risk into cash without giving up control. He frames *borrow, die, never
sell* as the tax-efficient version — margin loans against assets that are never
realised, so no taxable event occurs.

**Rush is imaginary** is the counterweight: most businesses have no network effect to capture, so there is no prize for speed. Growth-at-all-costs is choosing to take on debt you did not need. His example is Chick-fil-A's restraint against Boston Market. [BO_59sGxztY](https://youtu.be/BO_59sGxztY)

### 5. Allocation: downside mitigation over upside

The clearest statement of his own policy, from `Fy8XX8EuEnA`. Four buckets:

| Bucket | Role |
|---|---|
| Whole life cash value | A loanable bank account, ~4% guaranteed, structured like COLI/BOLI |
| B and C class multifamily syndications | Downside protection |
| Index funds | The thing he refuses to think about |
| Crypto | A ~5% hedge |

**The stated principle is that he deliberately takes inferior returns** on parked
money, because his upside is his business. *Make money on yourself twice*: take the
non-premium return from passive investments and get the premium return from your own
business, rather than trying to out-trade professionals.

**Expanded time horizon** is why this works — betting on a long enough horizon
virtually removes risk, which makes guaranteed-upside bets available to you that are
not available to someone who needs the money next year.

### 6. The cost of ignorance

Education is priced as an investment with a stated return, and the number is large
enough to justify almost any tuition:

- if you make $50,000 a year, ignorance costs you $950,000 a year — his framing of what not knowing how to make a million costs a $50k earner. [rp1PzCxj3eU](https://youtu.be/rp1PzCxj3eU)
- **The phlebotomy example** — $500 and a weekend → 3x earning power — is the cheap version of the same argument. [m-k0_pQJ1fY](https://youtu.be/m-k0_pQJ1fY)
- **He spent his own savings on it** — 100% of monthly income on learning at one point. [m-k0_pQJ1fY](https://youtu.be/m-k0_pQJ1fY)
- **The dinner that cost $350,000** — after $100K, $200K and $250K offers were declined, he wrote the larger cheque to buy a meal with a man worth $7B. [oDK4g5na4Jw](https://youtu.be/oDK4g5na4Jw)

**`ROIC` — return on invested capital** — is the correct denominator for these
decisions, and he puts it explicitly *above* LTV:CAC: not what it costs to get a
customer, but what it costs to build the whole capacity to serve them (buildout,
licences, recruiting, admin, tech).

### 7. Attention is the scarce resource

The tier's most repeated operational claim, and the one that connects wealth back to
business: money is downstream of attention, and attention is destroyable.

- **He paid nine months, 90 minutes every single day, to learn one thing — how to manage his attention** — and note what he bought with it: not strategy, attention. [6ZEZWuVC8-8](https://youtu.be/6ZEZWuVC8-8)
- **The anti-routine.** The routine that produced his best year was all subtraction:
  a new phone number, a fixed block, fewer inputs. Not a new system — a smaller one.
- **$1 in 1975 has the purchasing power of $62 today - a 6x difference in 50 years** — the inflation arithmetic that makes every static retirement number wrong. [6BQ3whjWG3M](https://youtu.be/6BQ3whjWG3M)

**`Know what your game beyond the game is`** is the warning attached: once the
business throws off more cash than you can deploy, you are forced into a second game
you did not choose — and learning it drains attention from the business, which is
where your highest return is.

### 8. Compounding vehicles

A structural feature that makes growth inevitable, rather than a growth tactic. Two
flavours, from `BO_59sGxztY`:

- **Customer-based** — customers who never stop buying (recurring or reoccurring).
- **Distribution-based** — for businesses with low repeat purchase (roofing, solar,
  real estate), the compounding asset is the *network of sellers*, not the customers.
  Prestige Labs' thousand locations selling each other's products is the example.

**`Jockey over horse`** is his largest investment criterion: given a market that will
not disappear, bet on the founding team's ability to solve problems in a dynamic
environment rather than on the idea. **`Theory of constraints`** is the single thesis
Acquisition.com invests under — a system grows until it is constrained and then grows
no further; find the constraint and attack it until the next one appears.

## Where this contradicts the other framework docs

Worth knowing, because the rest of this folder was built on third-party summaries:

- **`Theory of constraints` is not a scaling framework to him, it is an investment
  thesis.** The other docs treat constraint-finding as an operator habit; he uses it
  as the single filter Acquisition.com deploys capital under.
- **`Wealth is a ratio` inverts the goal-setting advice.** Most goal frameworks tell
  you to raise the target. He tells you to shrink the need.
- **Debt is framed as risk-reduction, not risk.** The third-party material treats
  leverage as danger; he treats taking money off the table as the de-risking move.
